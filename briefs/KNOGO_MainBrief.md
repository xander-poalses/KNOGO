# KNOGO DV300 and UX300 Capstone Master Brief

## Purpose of this document

This document combines the DV300 Theme 4 brief and the UX300 Term 4 Capstone brief into one working project brief. It treats the work as one capstone project with one problem space, one target-user direction, one research base, one product concept, one experience, and one final project story.

The two modules assess different parts of that project:

- **DV300** assesses the implemented application, the use of AI as a core feature, code, data, security, project management, version control, documentation, testing, and the ability to explain the implementation.
- **UX300** assesses the design position, manifesto connection, research, user understanding, problem framing, experience planning, iteration, interface design, prototype, case study, presentation, and the ability to defend design decisions.

This is a consolidation of the supplied briefs. It does not decide how KNOGO should pivot, define a new KNOGO concept, or replace the research and design thinking required for either module.

---

## 1. Combined capstone project

Create one original, research-supported digital product or experience that addresses a defined problem or opportunity for a defined user group.

The project must:

- respond to a real user or experience need;
- fall within a DV domain of social impact, accessibility, education, environment, or healthcare;
- connect to the student's UX Design Manifesto and show that connection through design decisions;
- use focused research to understand the context, users, behaviours, frustrations, needs, current solutions, and opportunities;
- define a focused problem, target user, project goal, scope, and design direction;
- include a digital interface or touchpoint;
- include at least five functional screens or pages for DV;
- include at least one AI service as a core product feature for DV, not as an optional extra;
- use suitable development tools, frameworks, data structures, and backend services;
- show the design and development journey from research and planning through testing, iteration, implementation, and presentation;
- be usable, explainable, and suited to deployment in a real context;
- aim for a positive and measurable impact;
- be achievable within the teaching and submission period;
- be presented as a portfolio project; and
- be defended through live presentations and demonstrations.

The intended result is one project with two evidence tracks:

1. **The product and implementation track for DV300**
2. **The research, reasoning, experience, and presentation track for UX300**

## 2. Shared project intent

The project should show:

- a problem or opportunity worth addressing;
- a reason the chosen user group would care;
- a clear link between evidence and project decisions;
- a product concept suited to the context;
- a defined MVP and controlled scope;
- a useful role for AI within the product;
- a designed experience that reflects the student's principles;
- an application that works;
- a process that includes feedback and iteration;
- an outcome that can be communicated to users, lecturers, and portfolio viewers; and
- a project direction that reflects the kind of developer and UX designer the student wants to become.

The project may be a mobile app, web app, platform, service, connected experience, speculative concept, or another suitable intervention. For DV, it cannot be only a console or terminal application because it must include an interface. The choice of format must follow from the target user, context, and intended impact.

## 3. Project principles taken from both briefs

### 3.1 Focus before scale

The project is not rewarded for being large. It should solve a focused problem and show control of scope. A smaller product with research, reasoning, working features, and finish is more suitable than a broad product that cannot be completed.

### 3.2 Research before solution

The project must begin with investigation rather than interface production. Research should be selected because it can answer project questions and support decisions. It does not need to be exhaustive, but it must be relevant.

### 3.3 One traceable line of reasoning

The work should show a traceable path:

> Design Manifesto and project values -> research -> findings and insights -> user definition -> problem frame -> project goal and scope -> experience planning -> wireframes -> testing and feedback -> design decisions -> implemented product -> final presentation and case study

### 3.4 AI must serve the problem

DV requires at least one AI service to form part of the product's main feature. The service must be selected because it suits the problem and product requirements. It should not be added to meet the brief without a clear role.

The student is not required to build an AI model. Existing services may be used, including services from OpenAI, IBM Watson, Microsoft Azure, AWS, Google Cloud, Google ML Kit, or another suitable provider.

### 3.5 User needs must shape the experience

The user, their context, and the task they need to complete must guide the product type, content, interaction patterns, accessibility choices, and feature priorities.

### 3.6 The project must show development

The final screens and application are not enough on their own. The submission must show research, alternatives, planning, feedback, testing, discarded directions, iteration, and the decisions that changed the outcome.

### 3.7 The student must understand and defend the work

Both modules require the student to explain decisions. DV includes code reviews and implementation questions. UX includes a design defence. The project should therefore remain understandable and owned by the student throughout its development.

## 4. Unified project process

## Phase 1: Direction and discovery

### Goal

Choose a project direction that matters, connects to the Design Manifesto, fits one of the DV domains, and can be completed within the available sprint.

### Questions to answer

- What subject, problem, or opportunity is being explored?
- Why does it matter?
- Which DV domain does it fit: social impact, accessibility, education, environment, or healthcare?
- Who may be affected by it?
- Which user group is the project for?
- What is the user's context?
- What change could the project support?
- Which Design Manifesto principles apply?
- How will those principles affect research, design, and evaluation?
- What does the student want the project to communicate about them as a developer and UX designer?
- Is the project feasible within the submission period?

### Research

Select methods that answer the questions raised by the project. Methods may include:

- interviews;
- observation;
- contextual enquiry;
- surveys;
- competitor analysis;
- precedent analysis;
- literature or secondary research;
- expert input;
- existing user feedback; and
- behavioural analysis.

Research should cover:

- the problem and its real-world context;
- the people involved;
- user goals, behaviours, needs, and frustrations;
- current tools, services, competitors, or workarounds;
- gaps, tensions, risks, and opportunities;
- suitable product formats;
- suitable AI services and their limits;
- development technologies and technical requirements;
- accessibility and usability concerns; and
- evidence that can be used to measure impact.

### Findings

Research findings should identify:

- patterns;
- tensions;
- opportunities;
- insights;
- changes to the first project idea; and
- decisions the research supports.

The project direction should not remain open after this phase.

### Shared outputs

- project title or working title;
- one-line project description;
- project direction;
- Design Manifesto connection;
- research plan;
- focused research;
- competitor or precedent review;
- findings and insights;
- defined user and context;
- early feasibility and scope decision; and
- references.

## Phase 2: Definition and planning

### Goal

Decide who the project serves, what it will solve, what the product will do, how the experience will work, and how the system will be built.

### Persona

Create a focused, research-informed persona for the primary user. It should explain:

- what the user is trying to achieve;
- relevant behaviours;
- needs;
- frustrations; and
- the context in which the user will encounter the product.

### Problem frame

Define:

- the user;
- the problem or opportunity;
- what needs to change;
- why it matters;
- the project goal;
- the intended impact;
- a problem statement;
- a solution statement; and
- a How Might We question.

### Product scope

Define the minimum viable product and separate features into:

- **Must have / MVP**
- **Nice to have**
- **Future consideration**

The scope should include:

- core features;
- at least five screens or pages for the DV implementation;
- user roles, where needed;
- the main user journey;
- supporting journeys;
- important states and system feedback;
- authentication, onboarding, and terms screens where needed;
- accessibility needs;
- CRUD requirements;
- security requirements;
- the role of the AI service;
- data and backend requirements; and
- measurable project objectives.

SMART objectives should be used where they help define success.

### Experience planning

Choose the UX documents that help explain the proposed experience. These may include:

- user flows;
- task flows;
- user journeys;
- experience maps;
- information architecture;
- service flows;
- system diagrams; and
- user stories.

There is no requirement to create every possible UX artefact. Each selected artefact should help make or explain a project decision.

### Technical planning

Document:

- the chosen application type;
- development languages and frameworks;
- the AI service and its role in the core feature;
- why the selected AI service suits the problem;
- frontend approach;
- backend and database approach;
- database schema or entity relationship diagram where a backend is used;
- data relationships;
- authentication and security approach;
- CRUD functions;
- technical risks;
- limits, costs, privacy, safety, or reliability concerns linked to the AI service;
- testing approach;
- deployment approach; and
- how technical challenges will be addressed.

### Brand and interface direction

Plan:

- product name;
- one-line description or tagline;
- logo or app icon;
- moodboards;
- visual references;
- colour palette;
- type direction;
- imagery and icon approach;
- copy and tone;
- style guide or UI kit; and
- interface principles.

### Wireframes

Create annotated wireframes for the parts of the experience needed to understand the solution. Annotations should explain:

- functionality;
- interactions;
- content decisions;
- hierarchy;
- user behaviour;
- system responses; and
- design rationale.

### Project roadmap

Plan the work across the available weeks and include:

- the MVP target;
- design and development tasks;
- research and testing tasks;
- milestones;
- code review preparation;
- feedback points;
- revision time;
- documentation;
- video production;
- final submission preparation; and
- regular version-control activity.

### Shared outputs

- persona;
- problem frame and statement;
- How Might We question;
- project goal and success measures;
- MVP and prioritised feature list;
- user roles;
- experience planning documents;
- system and data planning;
- AI service plan;
- annotated wireframes;
- moodboards and visual direction;
- project roadmap; and
- DV project pitch material.

## Phase 3: Design and implementation

### Goal

Turn the defined project into a tested user experience and working AI-driven application.

### UX design work

Develop enough of the experience to demonstrate:

- the main user journey;
- key functionality;
- visual hierarchy;
- interaction patterns;
- content;
- system states and feedback;
- accessibility considerations;
- the Design Manifesto in use; and
- the complete experience needed to understand the project.

The number of UX screens is not fixed. Quality and coverage of the core experience matter more than screen count. The DV implementation, however, requires a minimum of five functional screens or pages.

### Design development and iteration

Keep evidence of decisions that matter, including:

- design alternatives;
- interface explorations;
- discarded ideas;
- peer or lecturer feedback;
- usability tests;
- review findings;
- revisions; and
- changes in project direction.

The aim is not to document every small change. The aim is to show how evidence and feedback changed the project.

### Interactive prototype

Create a prototype that lets someone who does not know the project understand:

- the main journey;
- the core feature;
- key interactions;
- important states; and
- how the experience responds to user actions.

### Application implementation

Build the product using the selected technologies. The implementation must include:

- an interface;
- at least five screens or pages;
- the scoped MVP;
- at least one AI service as a core feature;
- working data display;
- suitable create, read, update, and delete functions;
- authentication or access control where the concept requires it;
- security practices;
- validation and user feedback;
- a suitable backend and database where required;
- code that is readable, modular, organised, and maintainable;
- suitable tests;
- version control through Git and GitHub; and
- documentation needed to run and understand the project.

### Project management

The project should use an agile approach with:

- an MVP;
- planned sprints;
- weekly progress;
- readiness for stand-up discussions;
- regular Git commits and pushes;
- meaningful commit messages;
- a clear branch strategy where branches are used;
- feedback records;
- recorded responses to feedback;
- progress demonstrations; and
- code-review preparation.

### Testing and review

The project requires:

- ongoing feature testing;
- code review;
- lecturer feedback;
- peer review;
- usability testing;
- a review form for peer testing;
- analysis of feedback; and
- changes based on findings where those changes support the project.

### Deployment

Deployment is encouraged in DV and carries a bonus mark. The application should be prepared for use outside the development environment where this is practical.

## Phase 4: Final communication and hand-in

### Goal

Package the project so that its problem, evidence, decisions, experience, implementation, and value can be understood and assessed.

### Final UI or digital experience

Produce final screens or digital touchpoints that show:

- the core journey;
- product features;
- content and hierarchy;
- interactions;
- feedback and system states;
- accessibility considerations;
- the visual system; and
- the intended product experience.

### Promotional video for UX

Create a short video that communicates:

- the problem or opportunity;
- the main idea;
- the value of the experience; and
- the final design in use.

It should present the project as a showcase, rather than record every screen or feature.

### UI device mockups for UX

Create three device mockups using devices that match the designed experience. The mockups should show a range of final screens without adding features that do not exist in the project.

Accepted dimensions stated in the UX brief:

- 7063 x 5008 px; or
- 1080 x 1080 px.

### Case study for UX

Create a single-page portfolio case study with a maximum size of 1920 x 24840 px. It must be submitted as a PDF and uploaded to Behance as a PNG.

The case study should contain:

#### Section 1: Hero header

- project title;
- app icon;
- short product concept;
- product mockup; and
- no student name, surname, student number, or term in the case-study header.

#### Section 2: Problem framing

- design challenge;
- problem statement and question; and
- solution statement.

The writing should present the project on its own terms. It should avoid phrases such as "this brief asked" or "I was tasked with".

#### Section 3: Research and design process

Show how research and planning led to the final solution. Relevant content may include:

- research methods and insights;
- features;
- persona and user research;
- flows, maps, or diagrams;
- sketches;
- annotated wireframes;
- style tile or UI kit;
- final visual design;
- feature explanations; and
- a QR code or link to the prototype.

Interface screens must be explained so that the reader can understand their purpose and function.

#### Section 4: Conclusion and final thoughts

Write from the project's perspective rather than as a personal reflection. Reconnect the final experience to the problem and explain how the digital interface addresses it.

### README for DV

Create a README containing:

- project overview;
- project purpose;
- feature summary;
- installation instructions for frontend, backend, and database where relevant;
- setup instructions for different environments;
- usage and navigation instructions;
- key feature descriptions;
- mockups or diagrams that add context;
- technologies used;
- team-member contributions where relevant;
- acknowledgements and references; and
- the DV demonstration video.

### DV demonstration video

Create a 5 to 8 minute product demonstration that walks through key features and user interactions. The brief examples include login, dashboard navigation, and CRUD operations.

The recording should:

- show only the app or code needed for the demonstration;
- exclude desktop clutter and unrelated windows;
- exclude menu bars where possible;
- avoid background noise; and
- be available as a backup during the live demonstration.

The DV submission checklist later states a 5 to 10 minute demonstration video. This conflicts with the 5 to 8 minute instruction in the detailed section. Confirm the accepted duration with the DV lecturer before recording the final version.

### Final presentations

#### DV live demonstration

Present:

- the project approach;
- the working application;
- implemented features;
- the AI integration;
- technical decisions; and
- requested code explanations.

Keep the demonstration video ready in case of technical failure.

#### UX capstone presentation and defence

Present:

- what was explored and why;
- research that changed the project;
- the user and their needs;
- the central design idea;
- the Design Manifesto connection;
- important design decisions;
- the final solution; and
- what the project communicates about the student as a designer.

The presentation is a defence of the work, not only a screen walkthrough. The student must be ready to justify decisions and answer questions.

## 5. DV300 hand-in requirements

## 5.1 DV project pitch

**Type:** Formative qualifier  
**Format:** PDF  
**Purpose:** Plan and justify the project before implementation.

Recommended pitch structure:

1. **Project title**
   - app name;
   - one-line description or tagline; and
   - logo.
2. **Concept and problem statement**
   - app overview;
   - chosen domain;
   - researched problem or need;
   - target user;
   - why the user would care;
   - proposed application type; and
   - why that application type suits the problem.
3. **Core features**
   - MVP scope;
   - SMART objectives;
   - must-have features;
   - nice-to-have features;
   - future considerations;
   - screens or pages;
   - user roles; and
   - user flows or user stories.
4. **Technical planning**
   - required AI technology;
   - selected AI service;
   - how AI forms part of the main feature;
   - tools and technologies;
   - reasons for selecting them;
   - expected technical challenges;
   - proposed responses to those challenges; and
   - database schema or ERD where required.
5. **Wireframes and UI/UX considerations**
   - moodboards and visual references;
   - mobile wireframes for main screens;
   - frontend framework or styling direction;
   - accessibility concerns; and
   - usability concerns.
6. **Project roadmap**
   - phases;
   - deadlines;
   - development plan;
   - progress and review points; and
   - version-control plan.
7. **Conclusion**
   - why the product is suitable and achievable.
8. **References**

The pitch must be concise, visual, persuasive, referenced, and submitted. Failure to submit results in 0% for the qualifier, even though progress feedback may still be provided.

### DV pitch assessment

- Brand conceptualisation: **20%**
- Concept planning: **40%**
- Technical planning: **40%**

## 5.2 DV progress milestone and code review

**Type:** Formative  
**Format:** Live check-in and demonstration, with an up-to-date Git repository  
**Timing:** Weeks 12 to 14 in the DV schedule

Students may be selected in any of the milestone weeks and must keep the project ready for review.

The milestone covers:

- implemented functionality;
- progress against scope;
- sprint planning;
- next steps;
- code structure and quality;
- tests;
- understanding of the code; and
- Git usage.

Peer usability testing takes place in Week 15. The student must create a review form, analyse the feedback, and use relevant findings in the final product.

### DV progress assessment

- Functionality progress: **25%**
- Sprint planning and agile execution: **25%**
- Code quality and organisation: **20%**
- Code understanding: **15%**
- Version control: **15%**

## 5.3 DV final AI-driven application

**Type:** Summative  
**Format:** Public GitHub repository, live demonstration, README, and demonstration video

Required evidence:

- working application;
- suitable development environment;
- at least five screens or pages;
- AI service used as a core feature;
- refined functionality;
- suitable CRUD functions;
- security and authentication where required;
- project and feedback records;
- code quality;
- Git history;
- README; and
- demonstration video.

Deployment is encouraged and assessed as a bonus.

### DV final-application assessment

#### Project implementation and completeness: 65%

- technology choices;
- implementation fundamentals;
- feature refinement;
- functional and screen requirements;
- AI implementation;
- suitability of the AI service;
- CRUD functionality;
- security and authentication;
- progress across sprints;
- response to peer and lecturer feedback; and
- deployment readiness as a bonus.

#### Application concept: 20%

- real-world context;
- significance and relevance of the problem;
- fit between product and problem;
- target audience;
- usability;
- feature fit;
- validation; and
- system feedback.

#### Project administration: 15%

- code quality;
- repository organisation;
- naming and structure;
- refactoring and development practices;
- version control; and
- README and video documentation.

## 5.4 DV industry insights and reflection

This is a separate theory deliverable linked to the DV module rather than the shared product itself.

### Presentation document

- landscape format;
- cover page with student name and student number;
- one paragraph for the main lesson from each industry speaker or talk;
- one slide for each insight;
- one pull quote from each talk;
- at least six insight slides;
- at least two reflection slides;
- career direction and goals;
- expectations, hopes, and challenges linked to industry; and
- reflection on the academic journey.

The document should act as a visual aid and should not be written as an essay.

### Video response

- 5 to 8 minutes;
- short personal introduction;
- key lesson from each speaker or talk;
- career goals;
- industry expectations, hopes, and challenges;
- one event from the academic journey that shaped the student; and
- closing thoughts.

Participation in at least three industry talks is part of the assessment.

### DV theory assessment

- Report document: **25%**
- Video response: **65%**
- Class participation: **10%**

## 8. AI-use boundary across the two modules

This section records the boundary stated in the briefs. It is not a judgement about the future KNOGO direction.

### AI inside the product

DV requires AI to be implemented as a core product feature. The service and role must be justified by the problem and technical requirements.

### AI used during project work

The UX brief allows AI tools for:

- interface microcopy, such as button labels, helper text, and onboarding copy; and
- image or illustration generation where the student generated the asset.

The UX brief states that AI may not be used for:

- research interpretation;
- problem framing;
- feature ideation;
- wireframes;
- UI layouts;
- case-study writing;
- reflective writing;
- analytical writing; or
- generating design solutions.

The UX work must emerge from the student's own:

> research interpretation -> problem frame -> planning -> design decisions

This means the implemented product may contain AI while the assessed UX reasoning and design work must remain the student's own work. Any future use of this master brief should respect that distinction.

## 9. Asset and resource rules

## 9.1 UX asset rules

Allowed:

- iOS 17 screen components;
- Material 3 Android screen components;
- royalty-free stock photography from the sources listed in the brief;
- AI-generated photography created by the student;
- the student's own photography;
- the student's own illustrations;
- AI-generated illustrations created by the student;
- one consistent royalty-free icon pack; and
- device mockups used for presentation.

Not allowed:

- stock illustrations, even where royalty-free;
- downloaded UI kits;
- app templates;
- downloaded wireframes;
- downloaded interface components from Figma Community;
- pre-designed app screens or layouts;
- AI-generated design solutions; and
- AI-written analytical or reflective content.

## 9.2 DV resource guidance

The DV brief permits suitable free-to-use or Creative Commons Zero resources and lists sources for:

- photography;
- icons;
- inspiration;
- mockup templates;
- AI services;
- machine-learning tools;
- version control; and
- Scrum.

Resources must be acknowledged or referenced where required. The brief permits AI-generated images if the model is referenced.

When one asset is used across both modules, follow the stricter applicable UX rule.
